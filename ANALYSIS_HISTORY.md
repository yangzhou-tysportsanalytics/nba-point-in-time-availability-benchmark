| Calibration of the bootstrap designs (2026-10-04) | Planned and run after a review argued that the two-way team bootstrap we had reported was miscalibrated. All headline results were known, under that design. The calibration is a placebo study on the real schedule with a known truth, so its reading rule needed no new judgement: a design either covers at its nominal rate or it does not. It confirmed the review on the mechanics -- the two-way weights give a variance of 2.66 against 1.00 and coverage near 1.000 -- and contradicted its diagnosis, because the per-game loss difference carries almost no team structure (0.3%) and what keeps the higher designs wide is the season. That is why the paper now reports both targets rather than simply narrowing the interval. |

| The interval each quantity is read under (2026-10-05) | Changed after all retrospective results were known, in the round that computed the calibration, and in the direction that resolves things, so the reasoning is worth stating plainly. The calibration is a placebo study on the real schedule with a known truth, and its two scenarios are the two targets the paper separates: redrawing only game outcomes is "in these four seasons", redrawing the team and team-season effects as well is "in an NBA season". Each target turned out to have one design that covers at its nominal rate -- home-team x season clusters at 0.960-0.963 for the first, seasons-then-clusters at 0.963-0.970 for the second -- while the two-way team bootstrap of earlier versions covers 1.000 in both and the dyadic estimator a previous revision had adopted covers 0.860-0.873. Both of those are now withdrawn. Because the two-way intervals were about half again too wide, a number of readings that had been null are not: the report's value is clear of zero in three of the four seasons rather than one, the premium on top of the opening price is clear of zero in both lined seasons with opposite signs, the paired cutoff contrasts clear zero rather than just reaching it, and under the plus-minus valuation the premium is negative and resolved. One reading went the other way and is the paper's own headline: the premium pooled over the four holdouts had excluded zero under the dyadic interval, and its lower bound is in fact zero to within the bootstrap's Monte-Carlo error, landing on both sides of it across five seeds. We report the point estimate and do not call it resolved under any reading. |
| A third valuation, at replacement level (plan P, 2026-10-09) | Added after every other result was known, including both existing valuations and the fact that they disagree on the premium's sign. The plan was committed before the code, the code before the run, and the script refuses to run unless the plan still hashes to the value it had then. Its five reading rules were fixed in advance and included the outcome we said we expected -- a premium still containing zero -- and what we would have had to write if the report's own value had failed to stay clear of zero, which would have been evidence against the paper's main claim rather than against the valuation. The run gave the expected outcome. What it adds is not a third number but an ordering: the three valuations differ in how much of the fact that someone else plays is already inside them, and both quantities fall monotonically as more of it is. One choice inside the valuation was fixed in the plan against our own interest: a below-replacement player keeps his negative value rather than being clipped at zero, because clipping would assume in advance that no absence can help a team. |
| The put-backs with a free coefficient (plan Q, 2026-10-09) | The test Section 4.6 had named as the obvious alternative and not run: each component of the difference between the two availability features entered as its own regressor rather than added into the feature at the valuation weight. Added after everything else was known, with the rules fixed first, and it changed one reading. The late Out designations survive at 0.069 against 0.070 at fixed weight, with the data choosing 0.87 of the valuation weight. The never-listed absences had been reported as a resolved negative; with a free coefficient the estimate shrinks and the Clark-West test puts it at p = 0.83, where a plain DM reading would have called the larger model significantly worse at p = 0.04. Since a nested model cannot be worse in population, that negative is the price of the extra parameter, and any suggestion that putting those absences back makes a model worse is withdrawn. One of the plan rules fired on its trigger and its conclusion was not written, because the conclusion had been mis-specified: the joint model is not compliant, so its agreement with the leaky feature shows the decomposition is complete and says nothing about the premium. |
| The strict six-hour sample as the paper's lead (plan R, 2026-10-13) | A choice of what to lead on rather than a new estimate, made after every result was known, so the plan fixed the condition first: the strict sample -- the holdout games where **both** teams had filed at least six hours out, with the same rule applied to the training set -- would become the benchmark only if the report's value stayed clear of zero there under all three readings, and the hybrid rule would otherwise keep the lead. It did: 0.185 (0.126-0.245) in these four seasons, (0.106-0.255) generalising, (0.101-0.265) with the season as the unit, and three of the four seasons clear of zero on their own. | The two tests of Section 4.6 under the other two valuations (plan S, 2026-10-14) | Added after a review objected that three point estimates lining up do not establish a mechanism and that all three had been computed under the valuation that makes the premium largest. The plan was committed before the code and then **corrected before any code was written against it**, because its first version named the wrong valuation as the one that reverses the premium's sign: that is the plus-minus valuation, not the replacement-level one. The correction widened the run to both alternative valuations and removed the assumption about which sign the rules would meet. One rule required the first valuation to be carried through the same new code as a control and refused to report anything if it did not reproduce the published figures; it reproduced all 25 of them exactly. The result went against the wording the paper then had. At replacement level the account holds with everything about a quarter smaller. Under the plus-minus valuation it does not: the premium is negative at every cutoff, putting the late Out designations back is worth 0.009 (-0.010 to 0.028) and reading the report an hour out rather than six is worth -0.002 (-0.014 to 0.010), neither resolved. What survives all three is the direction -- the premium falls by 0.150, 0.105 and 0.022 from twelve hours to thirty minutes -- so the claim that the put-back accounts for the premium is now restricted to the two valuations that charge an absence at the player's own output, and the claim kept in general form is that the later the report, the less hindsight adds. |
| What the second reading estimates, and a prediction interval (plan T, 2026-10-14) | A correction to a reported estimand rather than a new experiment, made after an external review pointed out that Section 3.2 defined its second target as "a season we have not seen, not an average over these four" and then reported two intervals that are both uncertainty of the across-season mean. The review is right. Predicting one season carries that season's own deviation from the average as well, which at four seasons is sqrt(5) = 2.24 times wider. The plan was committed before the code and its rule T3 was fixed in advance and against our interest: if the report's value's prediction interval contained zero, or excluded it by less than 0.01, the claim that the report's value is resolved under both readings would be withdrawn. It excludes zero by 0.0001 on the strict sample and reaches below zero with the fallback, so the claim is withdrawn and replaced by: resolved in these four seasons and for the average over seasons, not resolved for a single unseen season. The premium's predictive intervals are -0.270 to 0.362 and -0.246 to 0.359 and nothing is claimed from them. No published interval moved: the predictive reading is computed from per-season estimates the result files already carried, so the calibration did not have to be re-simulated. A second, smaller mismatch was fixed with it -- the season-as-unit interval is centred on the mean of the four season estimates while the point printed beside it was the pooled one, and a pooled RMSE difference is not the average of four season-specific ones; both are now printed, differing by 0.0022 and 0.0039. |

| A published interval corrected, and a claim withdrawn (2026-10-15) | An external review recomputed the season-level interval for the report's value by hand from the four per-season estimates and did not get ours. It was right. The standard error of the four-season mean was sqrt((tau2 + within) / n) with tau2 = max(between - within, 0): while tau2 is positive that equals sqrt(between / n), and once it clamps at zero it becomes sqrt(within / n), which is larger. It clamps precisely when the season-to-season spread is smaller than the sampling noise inside a season, which is when there is least evidence of real variation, and that is the case for the report's value. Its average-over-seasons interval was published as (0.091-0.300) from 4 October and should have been (0.144-0.247). The premium does not clamp, so its interval is unchanged, and every coverage number in the calibration is unchanged -- it was rerun under the same seed and reproduces them. The prediction interval added the day before inherited the inflated standard error, which was the only reason it reached below zero on the fallback sample; corrected it is (0.080-0.311). The claim that the report's value is not resolved for a single unseen season is therefore withdrawn, along with the places it had been written into. The same review's second point is accepted on its own terms: those intervals treat four rolling-origin estimates as independent, identically distributed and normal when they share training history and use windows of different lengths, and their bounds are analytic rather than bootstrap quantiles, so they are reported once as an illustration of how little four seasons settle. The arithmetic now lives in one module with the defect as its own test: a clamp that says no variation was detected must never widen an interval. |
| A claim about the premium's sign, withdrawn (plan U, 2026-10-15) | A cold referee read asked for the diagnostic the paper had printed for one alternative valuation and not for the other, and the answer withdraws a claim the abstract, the introduction, Section 6.2 and the conclusion had all carried: that the premium's **sign** depends on how an absent player is valued. It does not. That claim rested on a third availability feature in which per-minute box-score efficiency is replaced by per-minute plus-minus, described as a third valuation of the same absences. Plus-minus per minute is signed and team-dominated -- 60% of reference-rotation rows carry a negative value where 0% do under efficiency -- so a sum of it over a team's absentees is closer to a team-strength differential than to a loss. On the benchmark sample that feature correlates -0.03 with the feature it is supposed to revalue, against 0.89 for the replacement-level feature; fitted with the paper's own two controls it enters with the *opposite* sign, +0.146 against -0.109, so a side missing more of it is predicted to win by more; and it keeps 3% of that coefficient, -0.005, once the same measure summed over the whole reference rotation -- present and absent alike, carrying no absence information at all -- is controlled for, where the efficiency feature's coefficient is unchanged at -0.112 and the replacement-level feature's strengthens to -0.289. Whatever the third feature measures, the difference between its answer and the other two is not evidence about valuing an absence. The run is still reported, in Section 6.2 and in the two tests of Section 4.6, with the diagnostics printed beside it and no interpretation as a valuation. What survives is the weaker and real claim: the premium's *magnitude* is specific to the valuation, falling by about a third of itself between the two genuine ones, and the report's own value is clear of zero under both of them and under the third feature as well. The rule that decides this was written before the code and fixed against our interest: had the replacement-level feature failed the same three tests, the surviving magnitude claim would have gone too. It passes all three. Two statements in the 2026-10-05 row of this table are superseded by this one: the premium under the plus-minus valuation is reported as a run and not as a valuation, and the opposite-signed opening-price premiums are the subject of the next row. |
| Two seasons that were not comparable (plan U, 2026-10-15) | The same read found that Section 8's sharpest negative claim -- that the premium on top of the opening price is resolved in both lined seasons with opposite signs, 'a sharper failure to replicate than an unresolved one' -- compared two samples covering almost disjoint parts of the season, on the one quantity this paper shows runs with the calendar. Our archive holds an opening price for 647 of 2022-23's games, all of them October to January, and for 977 of 2025-26's, November to April; the paper's own month profile runs from -0.037 in October-December to +0.184 in March-April with the gradient repeating in all four holdouts. The sentence also gave 1,208 as its sample size, which is the closing-line holdout, not the 647 the opening-price result was computed on. Restricted to the months 2022-23 covers, the 2025-26 figure is 0.045 (-0.029 to 0.124) on 472 games rather than 0.105 (0.037 to 0.179) on 977, and does not exclude zero; on the remaining 505 it is 0.160 (0.056 to 0.264). The two runs also differ in whether the price product changes between training and holdout -- both of 2022-23's seasons are SBRO openers where 2025-26's holdout is DraftKings. Matched on the calendar there is no pair of opposite resolved signs, so the pair is reported as not comparable rather than as a replication failure. The section's conclusion is unchanged: it establishes that no model here beats the closing line and nothing about what hindsight is worth to someone holding a price. Only its stated reason changes. |
| The deployment gap under the design the paper designates (plan U, 2026-10-15) | The one quantity in the paper addressed to a buyer was reported under an iid game bootstrap, with the sentence saying the home-cluster design 'was never computed for this quantity' and that the iid one 'is the wider of the two available here'. The two available were iid and the withdrawn dyadic estimator; the design Section 3.1 designates for a four-season quantity is home team by season clusters, and it is wider on the side that decides the claim. It has now been computed, at 10,000 replicates over three seeds, in the released script as well as in ours: 0.068 (0.006 to 0.130) with narrow features and 0.100 (0.029 to 0.171) with wide ones, against the printed iid (0.015 to 0.121) and (0.040 to 0.161). Both still exclude zero, so 'clear of zero both ways' stands on a lower bound less than half the one printed, and the characterisation of the iid interval as the wider of what was available is withdrawn. The design for a statement about one unseen season reads (-0.030 to 0.161) and (-0.009 to 0.198) and contains zero, as this paper's season-level readings generally do. |
